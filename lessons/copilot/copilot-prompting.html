Prompting Copilot: a different dialect.

Generic prompting advice half-works on Copilot, because Copilot's superpower is grounding — pulling from YOUR mail, files, meetings, and people. The skill is telling it precisely which slice of your work life to stand on.

01 Ground every prompt in something

The weakest Copilot prompts float free ("write a status update"). The strongest ones are anchored to work objects: a file, a person, a meeting, a time range. Compare: "Write a status update" versus "Based on my emails with the Riverside team this week and Friday's project meeting, draft a status update for my manager — wins, risks, next steps." Same ask; the second one has somewhere to stand.

02 The four anchors

AnchorHow it sounds
Files"Using the Q3 proposal document..." — or attach/reference it directly in the prompt box (type / in many surfaces to pick a file)
People"...emails from Dana about invoicing..." — names scope the search to real correspondents
Meetings"...from Tuesday's ops call..." — recaps, decisions, and commitments are indexed per meeting
Time"...this week / since July 1 / last quarter..." — unscoped time is the #1 cause of mushy answers

03 Structure still matters — the Copilot version

The five-part prompt (role, context, task, constraints, format) applies, with one twist: context is mostly pointing, not explaining. You don't paste background — you name where it lives. A strong Copilot prompt template: "[Anchor: based on X]. [Task]. [Audience + tone]. [Format]. [Constraint: only use information from the referenced sources — tell me what's missing rather than filling gaps]." That last constraint is the Copilot-specific power move: it converts hallucination risk into a to-do list.

Verify like a pro

Work-grounded answers cite their sources — the files and messages Copilot drew from. Click one before you forward anything. Grounded doesn't mean infallible: it means checkable. (Full habit: our fact-checking lesson — the triage applies identically here.)

04 Iteration in-place

Copilot's drafts land inside the document, the email, the deck — so iterate there: "shorter," "more formal," "turn the middle into a table," "add the numbers from the attached sheet." And when an answer misses, the fix is usually a missing anchor, not a magic word: add the file, the name, or the date range and re-ask.

Try it now

Take a status update, recap, or summary you owe someone. Write it as an anchored prompt — at least two anchors plus the tell-me-what's-missing constraint. Check one citation before sending.
