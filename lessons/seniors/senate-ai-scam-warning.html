The Senate’s AI scam warning — and what to actually do.

On July 29, 2026, the U.S. Senate’s Special Committee on Aging held a hearing with an alarming name: “The AI Deception Machine.” The news coverage sounds frightening. This lesson walks you through what was actually said — and the good news the headlines skip: the same few calm habits still beat every scam they described.

What happenedA bipartisan warning from Washington

Senators from both parties — the committee is led by Chairman Rick Scott of Florida and Ranking Member Kirsten Gillibrand of New York — heard testimony from scam victims, a bank-security expert from the American Bankers Association, a fraud attorney, and a consumer advocate. The message: artificial intelligence is making old scams look and sound far more convincing.

Chairman Scott told the hearing that Americans over 60 lost $7.7 billion to scams in 2025. That number is why the committee met — and it’s why we’re telling you about it.

Why this lesson exists

Not to scare you. Every single scam described at that hearing uses the same handful of tricks — and once you know them, the pause that protects you takes about two minutes.

The tricksThe three things they warned about

1. Cloned voices

A few seconds of someone’s voice — from a video posted online, even a voicemail greeting — is enough for AI to fake a phone call that sounds exactly like them. One witness, a mother from California, testified that scammers cloned her daughter’s voice and staged a fake kidnapping. Over a terrifying phone call she transferred $5,400 before learning her daughter had been safe the whole time.

The lesson she wanted the country to hear: a familiar voice on the phone is no longer proof of who’s calling.

2. Deepfake videos

A Los Angeles surgeon testified that scammers built a fake video of him — his face, his voice — endorsing a health product he’d never heard of. If a video ad shows a doctor, a celebrity, or an expert recommending something, the video itself can be manufactured.

3. AI chatbots doing the talking

Scammers now use chat AI to run many victims at once — with perfect English, endless patience, and answers for every doubt. The old tell of clumsy wording and bad grammar is mostly gone.

The good newsThe defenses didn’t change

Here’s what the headlines leave out: AI makes scams more convincing, but not different in shape. They still rush you. They still scare you. They still ask for secrecy, gift cards, wire transfers, passwords, or the codes texted to your phone. Which means everything you may have already learned still works:

  • Hang up and call back on a number you already have — not the one they gave you. A real emergency survives a two-minute pause.
  • Agree on a family code word. Pick a word only your family knows. If a panicked call ever comes, ask for it. A cloned voice can’t answer.
  • Never pay with gift cards or wire transfers because a caller demanded it. No real bank, agency, or grandchild does this.
  • Never share passwords, texted codes, or account numbers with anyone who contacted you.
The video rule: Treat “amazing” endorsements the same way you treat urgent calls. Before buying anything a video recommended, look the product up yourself, or ask a family member — don’t click the link in the ad.

This week: set up your code word

One phone call is all it takes:

  1. Call your closest family members and agree on one word or phrase only you would know.
  2. Agree on the rule: any urgent call asking for money gets asked for the word — no exceptions, no hurt feelings.
  3. If the caller can’t say it, hang up and call the person back on their usual number.
